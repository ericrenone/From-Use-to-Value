# From Use to Value

### A plain-language guide to transforming any company with AI in 2027: one number to track, six things to fix, four quarters to do it

*November 2026. Every figure comes from published research, surveys, payroll data, regulatory texts, or disclosed incidents; sources are listed at the end. Scenes are composites and name no one.*

---

## Read this first

Almost every company now uses AI. Almost none has made money from it. The reason is not the technology, the people, or the budget. It is that companies have not turned what the technology can do into work that is redesigned, checked, governed, and measured.

This guide calls that distance the **absorption gap**, gives you one number to track it, explains the six things that close it, and lays out a four-quarter plan to do so. It is written to be read in an hour and used for a year.

**If you have ten minutes:** read the numbers below, the five stages (Section 2), and the plan at a glance (Section 10).

**If you have an hour:** read it all. It is built to be skimmed by headings and tables.

## 1. The absorption gap

### What it is

Capability is not the problem. The best AI systems now produce work that expert graders rate as good as or better than a human professional's on about half of real deliverables, roughly a hundred times faster and cheaper. They can work unsupervised for hours. The price of a fixed level of capability has collapsed. New frontier models arrive every few weeks.

Absorption is the problem. **Absorption** means an AI investment that is, all at once:

1. **In production** — not a pilot
2. **In a redesigned workflow** — not a tool dropped into the old process
3. **With a measured gain** — against a baseline you recorded before you changed anything
4. **Under governance** — with an owner, an evaluation that gates release, a defined human role, and an audit trail

All four. Three out of four is not absorbed.

The **absorption rate** is the share of your AI investment that meets all four, measured every quarter. It is the one number this guide asks a board to watch. Everything else here is a way to move it.

### Why the usual explanations are wrong

| The explanation | Why it does not fit |
|---|---|
| "The technology isn't ready" | Expert-comparable output on ~half of real tasks; ~95% on standard software benchmarks; capability doubling every few months |
| "Employees resist" | 80% of users report personal productivity gains; half of U.S. workers use AI at work; the largest worker survey found employees ahead of their organizations |
| "Change is always hard" | True, but the specific signature — high use, no enterprise value, systems that pass tests and fail in the field — is not generic resistance |
| "Leaders need to sponsor it more" | 90% of CIOs have dedicated AI budgets; nearly half are over them. Sponsorship is not scarce. Leaders' hands-on understanding of the technology is |

### The clocks you are racing

| Clock | Rate | What it means for your plan |
|---|---|---|
| **Capability** | Agent task length doubles every ~7 months (faster since 2024); expert-comparable rate went from ~12% to ~48% in 18 months | Anything you decide the AI "can't do" needs re-checking every quarter |
| **Cost** | ~$20 → ~$0.07 per million tokens for one capability tier in 18 months | Unit price is not your constraint; consumption growth is |
| **Releases** | New frontier models every few weeks | A plan built on one model is stale in a quarter |
| **Regulation** | EU high-risk rules bind 2 Dec 2027; transparency rules already live since Aug 2026 | Governance built in 2027 must be compliance-ready from day one |
| **Labor** | Entry-level hiring in exposed jobs 19% below trend and widening | The cohort with the deepest hands-on AI skill is the one you are not hiring |

The lesson: your own cycle time — how long it takes you to evaluate, redesign, verify, and govern — is now the binding variable. A twelve-month plan ships into a world that has moved three model generations. This guide runs on quarters.

## 2. The five stages of AI maturity

Companies do not jump from using AI to profiting from it. They move through stages. Most are at Stage 2 and think they are at Stage 3.

**Exhibit 1. The five stages**

| Stage | Name | What it looks like | Likelihood of enterprise value | Share of organizations, 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Access** | Licenses bought, policy published | None | ~10% |
| 1 | **Use** | People use tools for their own tasks; productivity self-reported | None at enterprise level | ~40% |
| 2 | **Insertion** | Tools embedded in existing processes; pilots running | ~6% | ~35% |
| 3 | **Redesign** | Workflow rebuilt end to end around the system; verification gates; measured baseline | ~32% | ~10% |
| 4 | **Governed autonomy** | Agents hold scoped decision rights in redesigned workflows, under identity, least privilege, audit, and a kill switch; absorption reported to the board | The high performers | ~5% |

Three rules of the stages:

- **The value is in the step from Stage 2 to Stage 3**, and that is the step most companies skip, because redesign requires someone who understands what the system does well enough to change the work around it.
- **Stage 4 is a governance state, not "more automation."** You can run fifty agents and still be at Stage 2 if they are ungoverned.
- **Advance one workflow at a time.** Two workflows at Stage 3 by mid-year and one at Stage 4 by year end beats enterprise-wide access every time it has been measured.

## 3. The six things that close the gap

Absorption fails when any one of six subsystems fails, and they fail together. Fixing one and not the others does not move the number. This is the core idea of the guide.

**Exhibit 2. The six subsystems**

| # | Subsystem | The question | What goes wrong when it is missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Authority** | Who decides about the technology, and can they evaluate what they decide? | Decisions ratified by people who cannot assess them; vendors win by default |
| 2 | **Workflows** | Which processes get rebuilt end to end? | Tools inserted; pilots that never scale; the 95% |
| 3 | **Verification** | How is output checked, by whom, with what capacity? | Systems that pass tests and fail in production; speed up, stability down |
| 4 | **Agents & governance** | What runs on its own, with what identity, scope, and audit trail? | Ungoverned agents; incidents; projects cancelled |
| 5 | **People** | Who does the new work; how are they hired, trained, promoted, paid? | Junior pipeline closed; new roles unnamed; training detached from work |
| 6 | **Economics** | How is value measured, funded, and defended? | Budgets blown; usage mistaken for value; verification unfunded |

They are listed in order of dependency. Authority comes first because the other five are decisions someone must be able to make. Verification comes before agents because you should not grant autonomy to something you cannot check. Sections 4 through 9 take each in turn: the problem, the evidence, the moves, and how you know it is done.

## 4. Authority: fix who decides

### The problem

The executive accountable for AI in most companies was chosen before the technology existed, on criteria unrelated to it, and holds decision rights over architecture, data, evaluation, and deployment that they cannot judge on the merits. The people who can judge those decisions are usually younger, newer, and lower in the hierarchy, because hands-on skill in a new technology concentrates in the people who learned to work on it.

Leaders do use AI — more often than individual contributors in the latest workforce data. But they use it for drafting and summarizing, the consumer surface. That builds familiarity. It does not build the ability to specify a system, read its evaluation, or know whether its output is right.

The result is familiar: the leader asks the team how to do it, cannot assess the answer, deliberates, listens to the vendor who speaks their language, and ratifies. The team learns that detailed advice reads as instruction and gets discounted. The program drifts with green dashboards.

### What the evidence says

- Organizations led by people with deep knowledge of the core work outperform; a boss's technical competence is the strongest single predictor of worker satisfaction, ahead of pay.
- Promotion systems select on prior-role performance, proximity, cultural fit, and credentials — none of which selects for skill in a technology that did not exist when the criteria were written.
- People without hands-on skill cannot reliably recognize it in others; they hire for the ability to *explain* the technology rather than *build* with it.
- Power reduces advice-taking. People with more power are more confident in their first judgment and less accurate as a result.
- Practitioners who have reviewed dozens of transformations now say leaders' understanding of the technology, not its feasibility, is the binding constraint — and the fastest companies make executives build their own agents.

### The moves

**1.1 Write a decision-rights charter.** For a defined class of decisions — architecture, data contracts, evaluation design, deployment boundaries, agent scope, technical vendor choice — the decision sits with a named person who can evaluate it, wherever they sit in the org chart. The executive keeps the problem, the budget, the risk appetite, the regulator, the customer. Overrides are written and reviewed quarterly. This is how hospitals and airlines already work.

**1.2 The leadership build.** Every executive accountable for AI personally builds, evaluates, and deploys one system on a real problem in their own area — about 150 hours. Not a course. A build. It changes how they hire, deliberate, and decide.

**1.3 CEO owns it.** Not sponsors — owns. Chairs the quarterly absorption review, has done the build, signed the charter.

**1.4 A qualified evaluator in every AI hire.** Someone who can ask: draw the system you built; what broke; how did you know the output was right. The executive judges fit and judgment and defers on whether the candidate can do the work.

### Done when

A signed charter has been exercised at least once; every accountable executive can describe what they built and what broke; no AI hire this quarter went through without a qualified evaluator.

## 5. Workflows: redesign, don't insert

### The problem

The most robust finding in 2026: companies that redesigned workflows were about five times as likely to report enterprise value as those that inserted tools (32% vs. 6%). The MIT review found the same — the 5% that returned value were integrated, workflow-specific systems.

Insertion is the default because it is the only change a leader without hands-on skill can specify. "Give the analysts a drafting tool" is easy. "Rebuild the credit-memo process so the system drafts, a validator checks against a defined test, the senior reviewer sees only exceptions, and cycle time drops from nine days to two" requires understanding the system.

### The frontier is jagged

AI capability is not a smooth surface. In a field experiment with 758 consultants, those using AI on tasks inside the technology's capability finished 12% more work, 25% faster, at 40% higher quality. On tasks of similar apparent difficulty just *outside* that capability, they were 19 percentage points more likely to get the answer wrong. The boundary is invisible, moves every few months, and differs by task.

So you cannot redesign at the workflow level. You break the workflow into tasks and place each one.

**Exhibit 3. Where each task sits**

| Position | Looks like | Design | Human role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deep inside** | Short, codified, verifiable, high-volume | Automate; audit a sample | Owns the evaluation |
| **Inside, near the edge** | Codified but longer, some judgment | System drafts; a person checks every one | Validator |
| **Outside, near the edge** | Needs context or accountability the system lacks | Person does it; system assists | Practitioner |
| **Deep outside** | Relationships, negotiation, regulated sign-off | Person only | Practitioner |
| **Unknown** | Not yet tested | Run the frontier test first | — |

Re-run the placement every quarter. The boundary moves.

### The frontier test

Before placing any task: take fifty real cases from last quarter, run the current system on them, and have someone who can judge grade the output blind against what humans produced. One week. This single practice prevents both big mistakes — automating what the system cannot do, and keeping humans on what it does better. It is almost never done, because it needs a grader who can judge, which is Section 4.

### Choosing the first workflows

**Exhibit 4. Six filters for a first workflow**

| Filter | Pass if… |
|---|---|
| **Exposure** | Most tasks sit inside or near the frontier |
| **Verifiability** | Output can be checked against a defined standard within the cycle time |
| **Baseline** | Cycle time, cost, error rate, and volume are measured now, or can be within four weeks |
| **Ownership** | One accountable business owner, not a committee |
| **Bounded blast radius** | Failure is recoverable and does not reach a customer or regulator without a human gate |
| **Value** | A credible path to a 20%+ gain in cycle time or cost, or a quality change a customer would notice |

Pick two. Not twenty. Typical 2026 candidates: support resolution with human escalation, software delivery with review gates, first-draft analysis with validator sign-off, document-heavy operations with a defined check, knowledge retrieval with a "time to find" baseline.

### What redesign actually changes

If a workflow cannot show all five, it is insertion:

1. **Sequence** — steps reordered or removed
2. **Roles** — someone becomes a validator; the senior reviewer sees exceptions only
3. **Gate** — release gated by a defined evaluation, not a manager's glance
4. **Metric** — cycle time, cost, error, exception rate measured per workflow, baseline kept
5. **Ownership** — a named person owns the evaluation, the configuration, the version history, the incident log

### Done when

Two workflows pass all six filters, every task is placed by a frontier test, all five changes are visible, and the baseline was recorded before anything changed.

## 6. Verification: check before you scale

### The problem

Generation is cheap and fast now. Checking is not, and most companies scaled the first without sizing the second.

- **Software delivery:** each 25% rise in AI adoption was associated with a 7.2% drop in delivery stability (2024); in 2025 throughput turned positive but stability still fell; about 30% of developers report little or no trust in AI-generated code. Telemetry shows why: unreviewed merges up ~31%, bigger batches, longer reviews. A change-failure rate going from 5% to 6% can erase the speed gain.
- **Customer operations:** the period's most-cited reversal — a payments company that said its assistant was doing the work of 700 agents, then rehired humans when satisfaction on complex cases fell in ways its dashboard did not show. The assistant still handles two-thirds of volume. The failure was the missing check on the cases that mattered.
- **Agents:** 42% of organizations saw a system pass tests and fail in production. Deployed agents succeed on about 57% of tasks.

### The rule

**Every unit of generation capacity needs a budgeted unit of verification capacity, sized before generation scales.** The verification budget has three parts:

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Evaluation sets** | For each workflow, a versioned set of real cases with known-good answers, re-run on every change and every new model, with a threshold that blocks release. Owned by a named person. The most important artifact in the transformation. |
| **Review capacity** | The human hours needed to check the share of output the placement grid says must be checked — computed from volume, staffed before volume rises |
| **Field monitoring** | Sampled audit of live output, with a trigger that pulls the system back to full checking if quality drifts |

### Three gates

**Exhibit 5. The three gates and the failure each one prevents**

| Gate | Checks | If missing |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-release** | The system passes the evaluation set on held-out cases | "Passed tests, failed in production" |
| **In-flow** | Every output near the edge is checked by a person before it acts; exceptions go to a senior reviewer | Backlogs, rushed reviews, silent approval of big batches |
| **Post-release** | Sampled field audit; drift detection; incident log | Quality collapses on the cases the dashboard does not sample — the reversal |

The in-flow gate is where companies under-invest because it looks like cost. It is where the value is protected.

### The human boundary, per task

| Setting | When |
|---|---|
| Person does it, system assists | Outside the frontier |
| System drafts, person checks every one | Inside, near the edge |
| System acts, person audits a sample | Deep inside |
| System acts autonomously within scope | Only with full agent governance (Section 7) and a kill switch |

Setting the boundary for a whole workflow — "humans check everything" or "the agent handles it" — is wrong in both directions.

### Done when

Every production system has a named evaluation owner and a test set that has blocked at least one release; validator hours were staffed before generation scaled; a field audit has run for a quarter with a pull-back trigger.

## 7. Agents and governance: earn autonomy

### The problem

Agents — systems that take actions, not just produce text — are the step to Stage 4, and 2026 was the year the governance gap became an incident log.

- 88% of organizations reported confirmed or suspected agent security incidents in the past year (survey of 900+ executives and practitioners).
- About half said an agent took an unintended or unauthorized action. One in eight could trace an agent's full path from instruction to outcome.
- In seven weeks of early 2026, researchers catalogued ten significant incidents rooted in ungoverned agent autonomy, including poisoned agent skill registries and prompt-injection chains into software supply chains.
- In July 2026 a major model-hosting platform disclosed an intrusion run end to end by an autonomous agent that spent about two and a half days inside its systems.
- In October 2026 a single attacker used off-the-shelf agents to breach more than a hundred company websites at roughly fifty dollars each.
- A leading research firm forecast that over 40% of agentic projects would be cancelled by end-2027, and that only about 130 of the thousands of vendors claiming "agentic" capability were real.

Companies that govern agents as identities with least privilege report incident rates around 17%. Governance is not the cost of agents. It is the difference between agents and incidents.

### The agent register

Every agent is registered. Not in the register, not allowed to run.

**Exhibit 6. Agent register fields**

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Identity** | Its own non-human identity and credentials — never a person's borrowed login |
| **Owner** | A named accountable person |
| **Scope** | The systems, data, and actions it may touch, enforced at the credential level |
| **Blast radius** | Worst case if compromised, and the recovery path |
| **Placement** | Which tasks, and where they sit on the frontier grid |
| **Evaluation** | The test set and threshold that gate its release |
| **Audit** | Where its full action trace lives, how long it is kept, who reviews it |
| **Kill switch** | How it is stopped, by whom, how fast |
| **Review date** | When scope and placement are re-examined |

Three in four companies say they have agent audit trails. One in eight can produce a trace. The register makes the first number mean the second.

### Autonomy levels

Autonomy is granted per agent, per task, in steps, and each step is earned.

**Exhibit 7. Earning autonomy**

| Level | The agent may | To advance, show |
|---|---|---|
| **A0** | Read and propose; a person executes | Proposals accepted at a high rate for a quarter |
| **A1** | Execute reversible, low-risk actions; a person reviews after | Post-hoc error rate below threshold for a quarter |
| **A2** | Execute within scope; a person audits a sample | Sample audit holds for two quarters; no scope breach |
| **A3** | Execute within scope including irreversible actions, within set limits | Blast radius formally accepted by owner and risk; any incident drops a level |

Most enterprise agents in 2026 belong at A0 or A1. The surveys showing 71% "in production" and 30% "handling most tasks without a human" describe agents at A2 and A3 that never earned A1. That is where the incidents come from.

### The agent-washing test for vendors

Four demonstrations, no slides:

1. **Show the trace** — the full log of what the agent did on a real task, readable by our audit team
2. **Show the scope control** — the agent denied an action outside its scope, at the credential level
3. **Show the evaluation** — the test set and threshold you used, and let us run it on our cases
4. **Show the kill** — stop it mid-task and show what state it leaves behind

A vendor who cannot do all four is selling insertion with a new label.

### The incident is a "when"

The companies that survive their first public agent incident will be the ones that can say, within an hour, what the agent did and why — from the register and the trace. The register is not governance paperwork. It is your incident-response plan, built in advance.

### Done when

Every running agent is in the register with every field filled; none uses a human's credentials; every agent is at a level it earned; the kill switch has been drilled; a full trace for any agent is available within an hour.

## 8. People: build the new roles

### The problem

Three facts show how far the people system has drifted from the work.

- **The entry door is closing on the wrong people.** Payroll data show 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-exposed jobs 19% below trend, with no gap for experienced workers and no rise in firings. Companies are not firing juniors; they have stopped hiring them, because the tools do the codified work juniors were hired for. The same cohort has the deepest hands-on skill with the tools.
- **The new roles have no titles.** Evaluation writers, validators, agent product managers — the work exists; the job descriptions do not. People do it informally, unpaid for it, and leave for places that name it.
- **Training is detached from work.** Employers expect 39% of core skills to change by 2030 and say skills gaps are the biggest barrier. Yet the largest worker survey found that people trained *as their work actually changed* were 3.3 times more likely to report value; generic AI courses delivered before anything changed did little.

### The moves

**5.1 Name and price the validator family.** Evaluation lead, validator, agent product manager: job descriptions, bands, and hiring against artifacts — an evaluation the candidate wrote, a system they shipped, a failure they diagnosed. Compensation benchmarks land in 2027; set them.

**5.2 Redefine the junior role.** Entry-level in an exposed function is no longer execution; it is validation. Entry requirement: something built with current tools, and the ability to say what broke. First job: check the system's output. This is how you build the experienced cohort of 2032.

**5.3 Train at the point of work change.** Reskilling follows the workflow calendar. When a workflow is redesigned, the people in it are trained on the new roles, with the new tools, on real cases, in the two weeks around cutover.

**5.4 Rewrite promotion criteria.** For any role that will hold AI decision rights: has this person built something on current tools, evaluated something, been wrong and learned why. One page per artifact. Without this, the authority problem of Section 4 reproduces itself every cycle.

**5.5 Turn reverse mentoring into a decision tool.** Two-thirds of the youngest workers already coach senior staff on AI. As practiced, it transfers familiarity and changes nothing about who decides. Redesigned: the junior partner gives a documented recommendation on the leader's technical decisions; the leader accepts or overrides in writing; the override log is reviewed quarterly.

**5.6 Measure psychological safety from the employee side.** Where managers made it safe to experiment, employees were 1.4× more likely to be frequent agentic-AI users. Leaders overestimate how safe it is: 81% of leaders felt safe suggesting new ways of working, versus 67% of employees.

### Scope, not headcount

The period's most-cited reversal was not an AI failure — the assistant still handles two-thirds of volume. It was a sequencing failure: headcount cut before the field audit showed which cases needed people. The CEO's own summary was that they went too far.

**The rule: automate scope, not headcount.** The placement grid decides what the system takes. Headcount follows the field audit, with the in-flow gate staffed. Announcing a headcount cut before the field audit has run is planning your own reversal.

### Done when

Validator roles have titles, bands, and at least two hires; the junior role in one exposed function is redefined with an artifact entry requirement; training is on the workflow calendar; promotion criteria are rewritten before the next cycle.

## 9. Economics: measure absorption, not investment

### The problem

AI budgets are big, growing, and over plan. Companies plan to spend about 1.7% of revenue on AI in 2026, up from 0.8%. The share putting more than 10% of IT budget into AI went from 23% to 74% in a year. Nearly half are over budget; one in six is on or under.

Three things make this wave's economics different:

| Dynamic | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Unit costs fall, total costs rise** | Token prices collapsed, but agents use orders of magnitude more tokens than chat, and consumption grows faster than prices fall. One large company reportedly spent its annual AI coding budget in four months. |
| **Models are a minority of spend** | Model usage is roughly 30% of total AI cost; integration, data, verification, and governance are the other 70%. Budgets built around model cost under-fund what produces absorption. |
| **Deployment is now a market** | Model vendors and hyperscalers committed about $10 billion in a year to "forward-deployed" engineers embedded in customers — their own admission that model access alone does not produce absorption. Useful for a first build; not a substitute for your own skill. |

### The value ledger

One per workflow, kept by the business owner, reconciled by finance every quarter.

**Exhibit 8. The value ledger**

| Line | Content |
|---|---|
| **Baseline** | Cycle time, cost per unit, error rate, volume — recorded before any change |
| **Investment** | Model and inference, integration, data, verification capacity, governance, training, partners — fully loaded |
| **Measured gain** | The same four metrics after cutover, plus the field audit's quality finding |
| **Absorption status** | Are all four conditions met? |
| **Net** | Gain minus investment — counted only if absorbed |

It does three things the usual business case does not: it funds verification and governance as investment, it stops usage from posing as value, and it rolls up to the one number the board sees.

### Five funding rules

1. **Fund workflows, not tools.** A license request without a workflow and a ledger is insertion.
2. **Fund verification as a fixed ratio** of generation spend, per workflow, not cut when generation is cut.
3. **Budget consumption, not price.** Forecast token volume under the redesigned workflow with agent multipliers; reforecast quarterly.
4. **Kill on the ledger.** No measured gain after two quarters at Stage 3: stop or redesign. The 40% cancellation forecast describes companies that will do this late and expensively.
5. **Use deployment partners for the first build, not the tenth** — and write the transfer into the contract: the evaluation set, configuration, and runbook belong to you.

### Done when

Every production workflow has a ledger reconciled by finance; absorption is reported to the board quarterly; verification is funded as a ratio; at least one workflow has been killed or redesigned on the evidence.

## 10. The four-quarter plan

**Exhibit 9. The plan at a glance**

| Quarter | Theme | Exit criteria | Absorption |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Q1** | Authority and baseline | Charter signed; leadership builds under way; two workflows chosen and baselined; agent inventory done; ledger opened | Baseline measured |
| **Q2** | First redesigns, verification first | Two workflows at Stage 3 with test sets that have blocked a release; validators staffed; field audit running | Rising |
| **Q3** | Governance and people | Agent register complete; autonomy levels enforced; validator roles hired; junior role redefined; promotion criteria rewritten; EU gap assessment done | 2–3× baseline |
| **Q4** | Scale, kill, cadence | One workflow at Stage 4; at least one kill on the ledger; quarterly cadence written into the management system | Board's primary AI metric |

### Q1 — Authority and baseline

- **Weeks 1–2:** Inventory every AI investment and score it against the four conditions. Inventory every agent, including ones IT does not know about and ones running on human credentials. The result is your starting absorption rate.
- **Weeks 2–4:** CEO convenes the accountable executives and signs the decision-rights charter. Where nobody inside can evaluate a decision class, name an external evaluator and a hiring deadline.
- **Weeks 2–12:** Every accountable executive starts a real build, reviewed at quarter end: what was built, what broke, what you now understand that you did not.
- **Weeks 4–8:** Run the six filters; pick two workflows; record baselines; run the frontier test on every task.
- **Weeks 8–12:** Open a value ledger for each, with verification capacity in the investment line. Set the verification ratio with the CFO.

**Q1 tests:** Can every accountable executive say what they built and what broke? Is the charter signed with a holder for every decision class? Do both workflows have a baseline and a tested placement grid? Does the agent inventory include the ones on borrowed credentials?

### Q2 — First redesigns, verification first

- **Weeks 1–3:** Build the evaluation sets *before* the systems, from real cases, with thresholds set with the business owner.
- **Weeks 2–6:** Redesign and build with all five changes. Staff validators before cutover. Use a deployment partner if needed, with the transfer clause.
- **Weeks 6–8:** Run the pre-release gate. Expect at least one of the two to fail the first time — that is the gate working. Log the block.
- **Weeks 8–12:** Cut over with the in-flow gate staffed; start the field audit immediately; train people in the two weeks around cutover; record the first post-cutover numbers.

**Q2 tests:** Did a test set block a release? Were validators staffed before cutover, with no backlog? Does the ledger show actuals against baseline? Was training at the point of work change?

### Q3 — Governance and people

- **Weeks 1–4:** Build the register from the Q1 inventory. Issue non-human identities; revoke human credentials; enforce least privilege. Assign every agent the autonomy level its evidence supports. Drill a kill switch.
- **Weeks 2–8:** Publish validator titles and bands; make two hires with a qualified evaluator on the panel. Redefine one junior role. Rewrite promotion criteria. Convert reverse mentoring into the override-log instrument.
- **Weeks 4–10:** Classify every workflow against the EU high-risk categories and sectoral rules; map existing governance to the requirements; schedule gap closure before 2 December 2027. Confirm transparency compliance in customer-facing flows.
- **Weeks 8–12:** Re-run the frontier test on both production workflows against the current model generation. Expect movement. Pick the third workflow.

**Q3 tests:** Does every agent have its own identity, enforced scope, and a trace within an hour? Is every agent at an earned level? Are validator roles filled? Are promotion criteria rewritten? Is there a dated plan for every regulatory gap?

### Q4 — Scale, kill, cadence

- **Weeks 1–6:** Take the strongest workflow to Stage 4: advance its agents on evidence to A2, and to A3 only where the blast radius is formally accepted. One workflow, not many.
- **Weeks 2–8:** Review every investment against the ledger. Stop what has not measured after two quarters. Close pilots that never entered the filter. Shut off agents not in the register. Expect to stop a meaningful share.
- **Weeks 6–12:** Write the cadence into the management system: quarterly absorption review chaired by the CEO; quarterly task re-placement; evaluation re-run on every model change; quarterly override-log review; annual promotion-criteria review. Build 2028 on measured absorption.
- **Weeks 10–12:** Report absorption to the board as the primary AI metric.

**Q4 tests:** Is one workflow at Stage 4 with a drilled kill switch? Was something stopped on evidence? Is the cadence written down with owners and dates? Is absorption the first number the board sees?

## 14. The one-page board brief

**Situation.** We use AI everywhere and have realized little from it. That is the norm: 89% use, 6% value, flat for two years. The constraint is not technology, willingness, or money. We have not turned capability into redesigned, checked, governed, measured work.

**The number.** Absorption: the share of AI investment in production, in a redesigned workflow, with a measured gain, under governance. Today: [x]%. You will see it every quarter, first.

**The plan.** Four quarters. Fix who decides and have every accountable executive build something. Redesign two workflows with checking built first. Govern every agent as an identity and rebuild the people system around the new roles. Advance one workflow to governed autonomy, stop what did not measure, and make the cadence permanent.

**The asks.** A signed decision-rights charter. Verification funded as a fixed ratio and protected from cuts. Titles and bands for the validator family. A redefined junior role. Promotion criteria rewritten before the next cycle. A kill rule on the ledger.

**The risks.** A public agent incident before the register is done. A 2 December 2027 regulatory deadline we meet by building governance now. A junior pipeline that, once closed, we cannot reopen. A reversal like our peers', which we avoid by sequencing headcount after the field audit.

**The test.** A year from now: absorption is the first number you see, it rose every quarter, something was stopped on evidence, and one workflow runs under governed autonomy with a trace we can produce in an hour.

## 15. Twenty takeaways

1. Capability is not the constraint. Absorption is.
2. Absorption has four conditions — production, redesigned, measured, governed — and they are conjunctive. Report the share that meets all four.
3. 89% use, 6% value, flat for two years. The gap is organizational.
4. Companies move through stages; most are at insertion and think they are on redesign.
5. Redesign is worth about five times insertion. Insertion is the 95%.
6. Six subsystems move the number and fail together. Fix one and nothing moves.
7. Authority comes first: put technical decision rights with people who can evaluate them, in writing.
8. Every accountable executive builds one system personally. About 150 hours.
9. The frontier is jagged: 40% better inside, 19 points worse just outside. Place every task by test.
10. The frontier test — fifty real cases, one week, a qualified grader — is the highest-return hour in the plan.
11. Pick two first workflows with six filters. Not twenty.
12. Verification is a budget sized before generation scales. Three gates; each canonical failure is a missing one.
13. More AI in software delivery raised throughput and lowered stability. Review is the ROI lever.
14. Nine in ten companies had agent incidents; least privilege cuts it to one in six.
15. Register every agent; earn autonomy in steps; test vendors with four demonstrations.
16. The junior role becomes validator, with an artifact as the entry ticket. That is how you build the experienced cohort of 2032.
17. Train at the point of work change — 3.3× the value of generic courses.
18. Automate scope, not headcount. Headcount follows the field audit.
19. Model usage is ~30% of spend; cheaper tokens do not mean cheaper AI. Fund verification as a ratio; kill on the ledger.
20. The EU high-risk deadline is 2 December 2027, and hiring systems are in scope. The deferral is build time.
